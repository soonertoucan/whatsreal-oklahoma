Our conclusion

Everything above this line is evidence. This section is what we make of it. It is labeled so you can skip it, argue with it, or check it line by line. You will not find a number here that is not sourced above.

Oklahoma is not a poor state because Oklahomans do not work. It is 45th in health, 48th in education and last in turnout because, for fifteen years, the people elected to run it have spent the money and the attention somewhere other than the hospital, the school and the paycheck. The record above shows where: a $4.7 million restaurant fraud under a no-bid state contract, $31 million in questioned pandemic school funds, $3 million for classroom Bibles, $252 million a year in private-school credits for families who were already there, and $3.2 billion in storm bonds on your electric bill for the next 25 years. None of that was a Democrat's doing. There has not been one in charge.

Then came the federal law every Oklahoma Republican in Congress voted for. It takes $6.7 billion from the state's Medicaid program while 63 of 73 rural hospitals are already at risk, hands the state a $240 million-a-year SNAP bill, and lets your health premiums rise 20% this year and 15 to 22% next. The men who cast those votes are on your ballot. Their challengers opposed the law. That is the clearest choice on the page.

For governor, the two candidates agree on very little and the disagreement is about money. Mike Mazzei would eliminate the state's largest revenue source in the same years the federal money disappears, keep the private-school credit, and has stated no position on the Medicaid expansion that covers 237,525 working adults. Cyndi Munson would keep the expansion in the constitution, end the private-school credit and send the money to public schools, pause the data centers that are driving your utility bill, and aim tax relief at the sales-tax and earned-income credits that reach a $25,000 family instead of $21 a year. On the evidence of what the last fifteen years produced, we think Munson is the vote for anyone whose family depends on a rural hospital, a public school, SoonerCare, or a paycheck under $100,000. If you earn well over that and send your children to private school, the arithmetic may run the other way, and the page shows you that too.

For Corporation Commission, the office that approved the storm bonds and will decide who pays for the data centers' grid, Rhonda Eastman would stop and study. Brad Boles would do what the Legislature tells him. The Legislature has not told him anything yet. In the 5th District, Jena Nelson has published positions on the cost of living, Medicare and schools and asked for four debates; Stephanie Bice voted for the law and declined all four. In the 1st District, John Croisant opposed the law; Mark Tedford has no federal record to judge. In Districts 2, 3 and 4 and the Senate race the incumbents' yes votes are the evidence, and the challengers have published little. Decide whether that vote is one you want cast again.

On the two state questions: 847 caps the property revenue that funds your school and county and does not promise to lower your bill. 845 removes the party-balance rule from the panel that picks your judges. Both move power away from local people and toward the Capitol. We would vote no on both.

None of this asks you to change what you believe about God, family or the country. It asks you to look at the bill. Honest weights are a moral issue too.

Talking about it with people you love

Nobody has ever been argued out of a belief by being told they are blind. Researchers have spent fifteen years measuring what actually moves people in political conversations. It is slower and kinder than a Facebook argument, and it works. Here is what the studies found.

Ask first. Then listen longer than feels comfortable.

Ten-minute conversations in which canvassers asked people about their own lives and listened without judging changed views on contentious issues, and the change was still measurable three months later. Conversations that only delivered talking points changed nothing.

Broockman and Kalla, Science, 2016; Kalla and Broockman, American Political Science Review, 2020.

Speak to their values, not yours.

Arguments framed in the listener's own moral language (loyalty, faith, family, fairness to people who work hard, care for the sick) are far more persuasive than the same argument in the speaker's language. A hospital closing is a question of caring for your neighbor. A SNAP bill handed to the state is a question of who pays.

Feinberg and Willer, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2015.

Facts do not backfire. Contempt does.

A study of more than 10,000 people across 52 issues found that correcting a false belief almost never makes people cling to it harder. What shuts a conversation down is feeling looked down on. Bring the number and the source. Leave the word "blind" at home.

Wood and Porter, Political Behavior, 2019.

Make it about their hospital, their school, their co-op.

People weigh what they can see. Which hospital would they drive to if the nearest one closed? Who in church is on SoonerCare? What did diesel cost at wheat harvest? The entries on this page have a copy button so you can hand someone the number and the source, not your opinion.

Ask them when and where they will vote.

Asking people to say out loud what time they will vote, where, and how they will get there raised turnout by several points in a controlled study. It beat every other message tested. Early voting in Oklahoma runs Wednesday through Saturday. Ask which day.

Nickerson and Rogers, Psychological Science, 2010.

One honest conversation beats a hundred shares.

Across dozens of field experiments, ads and mailers in general elections moved vote choice by close to nothing. What moved people was a person they knew, talking with them about something that touched their own life. You are the thing that works.

Kalla and Broockman, American Political Science Review, 2018.
